MASTER PROMPT
Create a Compact Thesis-Style Business Research Report
Transform the supplied Destination EV Charging business case study into a compact thesis-style business research report.

The final document should feel like:

Academic research × consulting report × infrastructure feasibility study

It must not look like a traditional 70–100 page university thesis, and it must not look like a generic startup pitch deck.

The target length is approximately 22–28 pages, including front matter, main content, references and a short appendix.

1. CORE PROJECT
Working Title
Destination-Based EV Charging Infrastructure in India

Subtitle
A Business Model and Feasibility Study for Converting Existing Destination Parking into Distributed EV Charging Infrastructure

The central research question is:

Under what conditions can existing destination parking be converted into economically viable EV charging infrastructure in India?

The study investigates a business model in which an EV-charging operator partners with businesses that already have parking — such as:

Supermarkets
Restaurants
Shopping malls
Hotels
Theatres
Offices
Hospitals
Universities
Other destination businesses
Selected parking spaces are converted into EV charging bays.

The study must evaluate the model critically rather than assume that the business will succeed.

2. IMPORTANT RESEARCH RULE
Preserve the evidence discipline of the original case study.

Every important claim must be classified where appropriate as:

FACT
Supported by a reliable source.

ESTIMATE
A derived value based on available evidence.

ASSUMPTION
A modelling input that has not yet been validated.

HYPOTHESIS
A proposition that requires real-world testing.

Do NOT present assumptions as facts.

Do NOT manufacture research findings.

Do NOT invent customer interviews, pilot results, revenue, utilization or market validation that has not actually occurred.

If evidence is insufficient, explicitly state:

Evidence currently unavailable / requires primary validation.

3. DOCUMENT STRUCTURE
Use the following compact structure.

FRONT MATTER
Page 1 — Title Page
Include:

Destination-Based EV Charging Infrastructure in India

A Business Model and Feasibility Study for Converting Existing Destination Parking into Distributed EV Charging Infrastructure

Then include:

Author
Institution
Programme / Department
Academic year
Date
Keep the title page extremely minimal.

Page 2 — Abstract
Approximately 250–350 words.

Cover:

Background
Problem
Research objective
Methodology
Major findings
Business implications
Key limitations
Do not write an exaggerated conclusion.

The abstract should communicate that this is a feasibility investigation, not a proven startup.

Page 3 — Table of Contents
Clean academic hierarchy.

Do not create an excessively detailed multi-level table of contents.

Use:

Introduction
Market and Literature Review
Proposed Business Model

Findings and Conclusion
References
Appendix
4. CHAPTER STRUCTURE
CHAPTER 1 — INTRODUCTION
Approximately 2 pages.

Include:

1.1 Background
Explain:

Growth of EV adoption
Development of charging infrastructure
Difference between vehicle growth and charging demand
Importance of charging location
1.2 Problem Statement
Explain the underlying problem:

EV charging infrastructure must be available where charging is useful, but dedicated charging sites can involve land, parking and infrastructure constraints.

At the same time, many businesses already possess:

Parking
Customer dwell time
Electricity infrastructure
Existing customer traffic
The study investigates whether these existing assets can be integrated into a destination-charging model.

1.3 Research Objective
Define 3–5 clear objectives.

1.4 Research Questions
Use approximately 4–5 questions.

Examples:

What portion of India's EV market is relevant to destination charging?
What makes a destination suitable for EV charging?
Can host businesses receive sufficient economic value?
What utilization level is required for viable unit economics?
What assumptions must be validated before scaling?
1.5 Scope
Clearly define what the study does and does not evaluate.

CHAPTER 2 — MARKET AND LITERATURE REVIEW
Approximately 3 pages.

Cover:

2.1 Indian EV Market
Discuss:

EV adoption
Vehicle-category composition
Passenger EV growth
Public charging infrastructure
2.2 EV Charging Behaviour
Discuss:

Home charging
Workplace charging
Depot charging
Public charging
Destination charging
2.3 Destination Charging
Define destination charging clearly.

Explain why charging location and customer dwell time matter.

2.4 Existing Research / Industry Evidence
Use the sources already contained in the supplied research.

Do not add unsupported claims.

2.5 Research Gap
Clearly identify what existing market information does not answer.

For example:

Existing market statistics establish the growth of EVs and charging infrastructure, but they do not by themselves establish whether individual destination sites can achieve economically sustainable utilization.

2.6 Conceptual Framework
Create a simple visual:

EV Demand → Destination → Charging Session → Host Value → Operator Economics

CHAPTER 3 — PROPOSED BUSINESS MODEL
Approximately 3 pages.

3.1 Concept
Explain the destination-charging model.

3.2 Stakeholders
Show:

EV DRIVER ↔ CHARGING OPERATOR ↔ HOST BUSINESS

3.3 Three-Sided Value Model
For each stakeholder explain:

EV DRIVER

Convenience
Charging during existing activity
Reduced detour
HOST

Charging revenue share
Potential customer engagement
Better utilization of selected parking assets
OPERATOR

Charging revenue
Location access
Lower land-acquisition requirement
Network expansion through partnerships
Clearly identify which benefits are proven versus hypothesized.

3.4 Customer Journey
Show:

Arrive → Park → Plug In → Charge → Use Destination → Unplug → Leave

3.5 Revenue Model
Explain:

Charging revenue
Host revenue share
Potential commercial structures
Operator economics
Do not invent pricing unless supported by the source material or clearly label it as an assumption.

Approximately 3 pages.

4.1 Target Customers
Segment:

Private EV owners

Drivers without reliable home charging

Host revenue share changes
Charger CAPEX changes
Charging price changes
5.4 Host Economics
Evaluate whether the host has sufficient incentive to allocate parking.

Important distinction:

Charging revenue to the host is measurable; additional spending generated by EV customers is a hypothesis until experimentally validated.

5.5 Three-Year Scenario Model
Include the existing directional model from the source.

Clearly label:

Scenario Model — Not a Forecast

Show:

Locations
Chargers
Revenue
CAPEX
Contribution margin
Cash requirements
Avoid false precision.

CHAPTER 6 — COMPETITIVE AND STRATEGIC ANALYSIS
Approximately 2–3 pages.

6.1 Competitive Landscape

6.2 Competitive Comparison
Use dimensions such as:

Network scale
Site acquisition
Destination presence
Charging speed
Host partnerships

6.3 Strategic Positioning
Explain how an independent destination-charging operator could differentiate through:

Underserved hosts
Site selection
Partnerships

Smaller distributed sites
Do not claim defensibility without evidence.

6.4 Location Strategy
Define selection criteria:

EV density
Traffic
Dwell time
Parking availability
Electricity capacity
Competition
Host willingness
Installation cost
CHAPTER 7 — VALIDATION AND RISK ANALYSIS
Approximately 3 pages.

7.1 Key Hypotheses
Create a table:

IDHypothesisEvidence RequiredValidation Method

H2	Hosts will allocate parking	Host willingness	Host interviews
H3	EV charging drives incremental host spending	Transaction data	Pilot experiment
H4	Sites can reach viable utilization	Session data	Pilot
H5	Unit economics can support host revenue share	Site economics	Financial model
7.2 Validation Plan

Phase 1 — Discovery

Customer and host interviews.

Phase 2 — Commercial Validation

Landing page and site economics.

Phase 3 — Pilot

3–5 sites.

Phase 4 — Decision

Compare actual results against predefined thresholds.

7.3 Risk Matrix
Use:

Probability × Impact

Include:

Low utilization
Host churn
Parking conflict
Charger downtime
Electricity cost volatility
Competition
CAC
Regulatory/grid constraints
Do not dramatize risks.

CHAPTER 8 — FINDINGS AND CONCLUSION
Approximately 2 pages.

Structure the conclusion around four categories:

What is supported by evidence
What is directionally supported
What remains unproven
What must be tested
Then provide:

Conditions for Viability

For example:

Sufficient utilization
Acceptable site acquisition cost
Sustainable host economics
Reliable charger uptime
Competitive access to suitable locations
Finish with a concise recommendation for the next validation stage, not a declaration that the business will succeed.

5. REFERENCES
Use a consistent academic citation style.

Preferred:

APA 7th Edition

Use in-text citations such as:

(JMK Research, 2026)

and provide complete references at the end.

Do not create fake publication dates, authors or URLs.

Use the strongest available source for each major claim.

  <span class="chapter-label">Abstract</span>
  <h1 class="chapter-title" style="font-size:24pt; margin-bottom:8mm;">Research Summary</h1>

  <div class="abstract-box">
    <h3 class="sub-heading" style="margin-top:0;">Background</h3>
    <p>India's electric vehicle market is expanding rapidly. Total EV sales reached approximately 2.3 million units in 2025, accounting for roughly 8 percent of all new vehicle registrations (IESA, 2025). Electric passenger vehicle sales grew 77 percent year-on-year in calendar year 2025 to 176,980 units (Autocar Professional, 2025). Despite this expansion, public charging infrastructure remains materially undersupplied. As of August 2025, India had approximately 29,277 public charging stations—a ratio of roughly one charger per 235 registered EVs, against a global benchmark of one per 6 to 20 vehicles (MMCM, 2026; Ministry of Power, 2025).</p>

    <h3 class="sub-heading">Problem</h3>
    <p>Dedicated charging infrastructure requires land acquisition, significant capital expenditure, and stable electricity supply. Many commercially attractive urban locations involve high real-estate costs that extend or eliminate payback periods. At the same time, thousands of commercial destinations—supermarkets, restaurants, malls, hotels, hospitals, offices, and universities—already possess parking, customer dwell time, electricity connections, and footfall. The central problem is whether these existing assets can be integrated into a viable destination-charging model.</p>

    <h3 class="sub-heading">Research Objective</h3>
    <p>This study investigates the conditions under which existing destination parking can be converted into economically viable EV charging infrastructure in India. It evaluates the proposed business model critically, identifying what is supported by evidence, what requires validation, and what assumptions carry material financial risk.</p>

    <h3 class="sub-heading">Methodology</h3>
    <p>The study combines secondary market research drawn from public regulatory data, industry analyst reports, and academic literature, with original analytical modelling of unit economics, scenario analysis, and sensitivity testing. No primary customer interviews or pilot data have been conducted. The absence of primary evidence is explicitly acknowledged throughout.</p>

    <h3 class="sub-heading">Major Findings</h3>
    <p>Unit economics are theoretically viable under base-case utilisation assumptions (approximately 6–8 sessions per bay per day), but remain highly sensitive to utilisation rate, electricity tariff, host revenue share, and charger CAPEX. Infrastructure concentration is a structural concern: approximately 60 percent of current public chargers are located in five states (Ministry of Power, 2025). Site-level utilisation as low as 5 percent has been observed at deployed public chargers (MMCM, 2026), indicating that proximity to EV demand does not automatically generate sessions. Competitive pressure from large-balance-sheet operators—Tata Power, ChargeZone, Statiq, and Jio-bp—is material and intensifying.</p>

    <h3 class="sub-heading">Business Implications</h3>
    <p>The destination-charging model addresses a genuine infrastructure gap and avoids the land-acquisition cost faced by dedicated operators. The model is directionally promising, but viability depends on achieving and sustaining minimum utilisation thresholds that have not yet been demonstrated at the site level. A structured pilot programme across 3–5 heterogeneous destination types is the recommended next step before any scaling decision.</p>

    <h3 class="sub-heading">Key Limitations</h3>
    <p>No primary validation has been conducted. Customer willingness to charge at destinations, host willingness to allocate parking, and incremental spending uplift attributable to EV charging remain unproven hypotheses. All financial projections should be treated as directional modelling, not forecasts.</p>

  <h1 class="chapter-title">Introduction</h1>

  <h2 class="section-heading">1.1 Background</h2>
  <p>India is in the early-to-mid phase of an electric vehicle transition. <span class="badge badge-fact">Fact</span> Total EV registrations reached approximately 2.3 million units in 2025, representing 8 percent of new vehicle sales (IESA, 2025). Electric passenger vehicle sales grew 77 percent year-on-year in CY2025 to 176,980 units—the segment's strongest annual performance on record (Autocar Professional, 2025). Government policy has been a primary driver: the FAME scheme (2015), the PM E-DRIVE scheme (2024), and the NEV Policy (2024) have collectively channelled over ₹2,000 crore toward charging infrastructure (Ministry of Power, 2024; IBEF, 2026). The government target of 30 percent EV penetration by FY2030 implies a compound growth requirement well above current trajectory (S&P Global Mobility, 2025).</p>

  <p>The vehicle fleet, however, is growing faster than the infrastructure to serve it. <span class="badge badge-fact">Fact</span> Public charging stations expanded from 5,151 in December 2022 to approximately 29,277 by August 2025—a near-sixfold increase in under three years (Bolt.Earth, 2025; Ministry of Power, 2025). Despite this, India's charger-to-EV ratio stands at approximately 1:235 against a widely referenced global benchmark of 1:6 to 1:20 (MMCM, 2026). Achieving the government's 2030 EV target would require an estimated 1.32 million public charging stations—more than 40 times the current installed base (ORF, cited in MMCM, 2026).</p>

  <h2 class="section-heading">1.2 Problem Statement</h2>
  <p>Dedicated charging infrastructure faces several structural constraints. Land in urban commercial zones commands significant cost: Mumbai's business districts report leases of approximately ₹200 per sq ft per month, consuming up to 40 percent of project budgets and extending payback periods (Mordor Intelligence, 2025). Charger downtime is an operational concern: a national non-functional rate of 18 percent has been observed, with some networks reporting far worse figures—BPCL's network recorded 60 percent non-operational units; site utilisation as low as 5 percent has been documented at deployed public stations (MMCM, 2026; Mordor Intelligence, 2025).</p>

  <p>These constraints create a structural opportunity for an alternative model: rather than acquiring new sites, an operator could partner with existing destination businesses—supermarkets, restaurants, malls, hotels, hospitals, universities—that already possess parking inventory, dwell time, customer traffic, and electricity connections. The question this study investigates is whether this integration is economically viable at the site level, and under what specific conditions.</p>

  <h2 class="section-heading">2.3 Destination Charging — Definition and Rationale</h2>
  <p>Destination charging refers to EV charging infrastructure installed at locations where users arrive with a primary purpose other than charging—shopping, dining, receiving medical care, working, or staying overnight. The charging session is incidental to the primary activity; the charger is accessed during time the user would have spent at the location regardless.</p>

  <p>This model is structurally distinct from both home charging and highway fast charging. Its value proposition rests on <em>dwell time alignment</em>: AC chargers operating at 7–22 kW can provide meaningful range addition during the 45 minutes to several hours a customer spends at a destination. A 22 kW AC charger delivering 2–3 hours of charge can restore 30–50 km of range, which is sufficient for most urban use cases.</p>

  <h2 class="section-heading">3.1 Concept</h2>
  <p>The model proposes that an independent EV charging operator partners with commercial destination businesses. The operator supplies, installs, operates, and maintains AC charging equipment in selected bays within the host's existing parking area. The host provides land, electricity access, and customer footfall. Revenue from charging sessions is shared between the operator and the host according to a pre-agreed commercial structure.</p>

  <p>The core insight is asset synthesis: the operator acquires location access without land acquisition costs; the host monetises underutilised parking without operational complexity. Neither party can achieve the model's full value independently — the operator lacks location, the host lacks charging expertise.</p>

  <h2 class="section-heading">3.5 Revenue Model</h2>
  <p><span class="badge badge-assumption">Assumption</span> The operator charges drivers per kWh consumed. Observed public AC charging rates in India cluster at ₹8–₹15 per kWh (Pulse Energy, 2025). The operator pays the host's electricity cost at the applicable commercial tariff (₹6–₹15 per kWh depending on state and load category) plus a revenue share agreed with the host. The spread between the consumer-facing price and total cost — electricity + host share + maintenance + software — determines the operator's contribution margin per session.</p>

  <p>Alternative commercial structures include a fixed monthly access fee paid to the host (providing host with income certainty) or a hybrid of fixed fee plus variable share. The optimal structure depends on the host's preference for income certainty versus upside participation and on the operator's utilisation confidence at each site.</p>

  <h2 class="section-heading">5.4 Host Economics</h2>
  <p>The host must evaluate the economic trade-off between allocating a parking bay to EV charging versus leaving it available for general parking. This trade-off depends on the host's existing parking utilisation, the opportunity cost of the bay, and the revenue share received from charging sessions.</p>

  <div class="two-col">
    <div>
      <h3 class="sub-heading" style="margin-top:0;">What the host receives — measurable</h3>
      <div class="benefit-item"><strong>Charging revenue share</strong> — 15–25% of session revenue (₹47K–₹79K annually at base case, per bay) <span class="badge badge-estimate">Estimate</span></div>

      <div class="benefit-item" style="margin-top:2mm;"><strong>No operational management</strong> — Operator manages monitoring, maintenance, and billing</div>
    </div>
    <div>
      <h3 class="sub-heading" style="margin-top:0;">What the host receives — unproven</h3>
      <div class="benefit-item"><strong>Incremental customer spending</strong> — EV owners may spend more time and money at the destination while charging. <strong>This is a hypothesis. Transaction data required to validate.</strong> <span class="badge badge-hypothesis">Hypothesis</span></div>
      <div class="benefit-item" style="margin-top:2mm;"><strong>EV-owner customer acquisition</strong> — Positioning as EV-friendly may attract higher-income customers in early adoption phase. <span class="badge badge-hypothesis">Hypothesis</span></div>

  <div style="margin:5mm 0; border:0.4pt solid var(--risk); padding:4mm 5mm; background:rgba(166,86,75,0.04);">
    <span class="label" style="color:var(--risk); display:block; margin-bottom:2mm;">Critical Assumption — Host Incentive</span>
    <p style="margin:0; font-size:9.5pt;">The host's decision to allocate parking to EV charging rests primarily on the measurable revenue share from sessions. In low-utilisation scenarios, this share may be insufficient to justify giving up a general parking bay, particularly at destinations with high parking demand (e.g., popular malls on weekends). The operator must demonstrate sufficient session volume — or offer a guaranteed minimum payment — to create a durable commercial relationship with the host. This condition has not been tested.</p>

  <h2 class="section-heading">6.3 Strategic Positioning</h2>

    </div>
    <div class="phase-cell">

  <h2 class="section-heading">What Is Supported by Evidence</h2>

  <div class="two-col">
    <div class="stat-block" style="margin:0;">
      <div style="font-size:8pt; font-weight:700; text-transform:uppercase; letter-spacing:0.08em; color:var(--sage); margin-bottom:2mm;">EV Market Growth</div>
      <p style="font-size:9.5pt; margin:0;">India's passenger EV fleet grew 77% in CY2025. The total EV market is on a documented growth trajectory. Charging infrastructure demand will increase.</p>
      <div style="margin-top:2mm;"><span class="badge badge-fact">Fact</span></div>
    </div>
    <div class="stat-block" style="margin:0;">
      <div style="font-size:8pt; font-weight:700; text-transform:uppercase; letter-spacing:0.08em; color:var(--sage); margin-bottom:2mm;">Infrastructure Gap</div>
      <p style="font-size:9.5pt; margin:0;">India's charger-to-EV ratio (1:235) is critically below global benchmarks. Reaching 2030 targets requires 40× expansion of the current base.</p>
      <div style="margin-top:2mm;"><span class="badge badge-fact">Fact</span></div>
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="two-col" style="margin-top:3mm;">
    <div class="stat-block" style="margin:0;">
      <div style="font-size:8pt; font-weight:700; text-transform:uppercase; letter-spacing:0.08em; color:var(--sage); margin-bottom:2mm;">Land Cost Problem</div>
      <p style="font-size:9.5pt; margin:0;">Urban land costs absorb up to 40% of project budgets for dedicated charging operators. Destination-site access avoids this cost.</p>
      <div style="margin-top:2mm;"><span class="badge badge-fact">Fact</span></div>
    </div>
    <div class="stat-block" style="margin:0;">
      <div style="font-size:8pt; font-weight:700; text-transform:uppercase; letter-spacing:0.08em; color:var(--sage); margin-bottom:2mm;">Policy Alignment</div>
      <p style="font-size:9.5pt; margin:0;">Ministry of Power guidelines explicitly mandate and encourage charger installation at workplaces, malls, and residential complexes. The destination model is policy-aligned.</p>
      <div style="margin-top:2mm;"><span class="badge badge-fact">Fact</span></div>
    </div>
  </div>

  <h2 class="section-heading" style="margin-top:6mm;">What Is Directionally Supported</h2>
  <div class="finding-block">
    <div class="finding-text">At base-case utilisation (6 sessions/bay/day), unit economics are positive.</div>
    <div class="finding-sub">The economic model produces a positive annual contribution margin of approximately ₹74,000 per bay at base-case assumptions, with a CAPEX payback of approximately 4 years. This is directionally viable — but depends entirely on achieving utilisation that has not been demonstrated in this study.</div>
  </div>

  <div class="finding-block">
    <div class="finding-text">The destination model structurally reduces acquisition cost relative to dedicated charging sites.</div>
    <div class="finding-sub">By avoiding land acquisition and leveraging hosts' existing electricity infrastructure, the model's CAPEX per bay is materially lower than a standalone public charging station. This structural advantage is real, though its magnitude depends on site-specific electrical upgrade requirements.</div>
  </div>

  <h2 class="section-heading">Conditions for Viability</h2>

  <div class="conditions-grid">
    <div class="condition-item">
      <span class="condition-icon">01 — Utilisation</span>
      <div class="condition-text">Sites must achieve ≥5.5 sessions per bay per day within 6–12 months of deployment. Below this threshold, the base-case model does not produce positive contribution.</div>
    </div>
    <div class="condition-item">
      <span class="condition-icon">02 — Host Commitment</span>
      <div class="condition-text">Host businesses must commit minimum 12-month contracts and actively support EV bay availability (enforcement against ICE-blocking). Passive hosts reduce utilisation.</div>
    </div>
    <div class="condition-item">
      <span class="condition-icon">03 — Charger Uptime</span>
      <div class="condition-text">Network must sustain ≥90% charger uptime. The documented national non-functional rate of 18% represents a customer trust risk that must not be replicated.</div>
    </div>
    <div class="condition-item">
      <span class="condition-icon">04 — Electricity Economics</span>
      <div class="condition-text">The spread between commercial electricity purchase cost and consumer charging price must be preserved. If tariffs rise above ₹11/kWh while consumer prices are capped, margin disappears.</div>
    </div>
    <div class="condition-item">
      <span class="condition-icon">05 — Competitive Access</span>
      <div class="condition-text">The operator must secure destination sites before large-balance-sheet competitors (Tata Power, Statiq) onboard the same locations. This window is finite and shrinking.</div>
    </div>
    <div class="condition-item">
      <span class="condition-icon">06 — Site Selection Discipline</span>
      <div class="condition-text">Not all destinations are viable. The operator must maintain rigorous site selection criteria and exit low-performing sites before they consume disproportionate operational resources.</div>
    </div>

  <h2 class="section-heading">Recommendation</h2>
  <div style="padding:7mm; border:1pt solid var(--sage); background:rgba(104,124,112,0.06); margin:4mm 0;">
    <p style="font-size:12pt; font-weight:600; color:var(--text); line-height:1.45; margin:0;">The destination-charging model is structurally coherent and addresses a documented infrastructure gap in India's EV ecosystem. The economics are plausible under base-case assumptions. However, the central assumptions — that EV drivers will use destination charging regularly, that hosts will allocate parking at acceptable revenue share terms, and that sites can achieve minimum viable utilisation — have not been validated. No scaling decision should be made on the basis of this analysis alone.</p>
    <p style="margin-top:4mm; font-size:10pt; color:var(--secondary); margin-bottom:0;">The recommended next step is a structured, time-bounded validation programme (Phase 1–3 as described in Chapter 7) before any multi-site capital commitment. The programme should be designed to produce a definitive Go / No-Go / Pivot decision within 10–12 months, with pre-defined decision thresholds that are not adjusted after the data is collected.</p>
